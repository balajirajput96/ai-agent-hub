## Evidence map

| Classification                 | Verified statement permitted in the reel                                                                                                                                                               | Boundary                                                                                                                                                                   |
| ------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Established research construct | Affective forecasting is the attempt to predict one’s future emotional reaction to an event.                                                                                                           | It does not mean that every emotional prediction is inaccurate.                                                                                                            |
| Mixed empirical pattern        | In many experimental settings, participants predict more intense general emotional reactions to a focal future event than they later report experiencing. This pattern is often called an impact bias. | The magnitude and even interpretation of the pattern depend on what is asked, when it is asked, and whether people report feelings about the event or feelings in general. |
| Mechanistic interpretation     | Focalism is one proposed account: when forecasting, people may focus heavily on one salient event and underweight other future experiences.                                                            | This is not proof that a particular person is irrational, emotionally weak, or unable to make good decisions.                                                              |
| Methodological boundary        | A peer-reviewed reexamination and meta-analysis found that apparent overestimation can partly reflect a mismatch between forecasting questions and later experience questions.                         | Do not present “you always recover faster than expected” as a law, and do not use the effect to dismiss grief, trauma, loss, pain, or meaningful emotions.                 |
| Safe expert interpretation     | Before a major choice, adding the question “What else will still occupy my day?” can illustrate broader context without promising a better decision.                                                   | This is an educational reflection prompt, not financial, relationship, medical, legal, or mental-health advice.                                                            |
| Excluded claims                | Guaranteed resilience, predicting another person’s feelings, choice optimization, clinical treatment, and claims about how long any individual’s emotion will last.                                    | Excluded because the cited research does not justify individual forecasts or prescriptive life decisions.                                                                  |
| Philosophy/spirituality        | No philosophical or spiritual claim is used.                                                                                                                                                           | None should be presented as empirical evidence.                                                                                                                            |

## Sources

1. Hoerger M, Quirk SW, Lucas RE, Carr TH. _Cognitive determinants of affective forecasting errors._ Judgment and Decision Making, 2010;5(5):365–373. The repeated-measures study discusses impact bias, focalism, contextual variables, and individual differences; it also emphasizes that predicted and actual reactions should be assessed distinctly. [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3170528/).
2. Levine LJ, Lench HC, Kaplan RL, Safer MA. _Accuracy and artifact: Reexamining the intensity bias in affective forecasting._ Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 2012;103(4):584–605. The paper includes a meta-analysis and reports that question framing and a mismatch between predicted and later reported feelings can partly contribute to apparent overestimation. [Open manuscript](https://escholarship.org/content/qt1cq347f8/qt1cq347f8.pdf) and [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0029544).
3. Liu L, Sun W, Fang P, Jiang Y, Tian L. _Be optimistic or be cautious? Affective forecasting bias in allocation decisions and its effect._ Frontiers in Psychology, 2022;13:1026557. A laboratory allocation study found forecast–experience differences in that setting; it is used only as an example of a context-specific design. [Full text](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.1026557).

## Evidence map

| Classification                 | Verified statement permitted in the reel                                                                                                                                                             | Boundary                                                                                                        |
| ------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Established laboratory finding | In rapid streams of distractors, reporting a second target can be reduced when it appears roughly 200–500 milliseconds after a first target that must also be processed.                             | This describes performance in a specific laboratory task, not a claim that people generally “cannot multitask.” |
| Established laboratory finding | The effect depends on task structure: the same review literature describes “lag-1 sparing” when the second target immediately follows the first in some rapid-serial-visual-presentation conditions. | Do not state that the second target is always missed or that there is a fixed universal time window.            |
| Mixed/mechanistic evidence     | Reviews link the effect to a combination of attentional engagement and working-memory encoding limitations.                                                                                          | The exact mechanism remains debated; a simple “brain shuts down” explanation is not permitted.                  |
| Expert/review interpretation   | The paradigm helps researchers study temporal limits of selective attention.                                                                                                                         | It is not a personal test, a diagnosis, or proof of a particular individual’s attention ability.                |
| Not used                       | ADHD, mental-health, driving safety, productivity, treatment, training, or medication claims.                                                                                                        | Excluded to avoid turning a laboratory phenomenon into health or real-world advice.                             |
| Philosophy/spirituality        | No philosophical or spiritual claim is used.                                                                                                                                                         | None should be presented as cognitive neuroscience.                                                             |

## Sources

1. Dux PE, Marois R. _How humans search for targets through time: A review of data and theory from the attentional blink._ Attention, Perception, & Psychophysics, 2009;71:1683–1700. The review describes reduced second-target report in rapid serial visual presentation when the second target appears around 200–500 ms after the first target. [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2915904/) and [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3758/APP.71.8.1683).
2. Zivony A, Lamy D. _What processes are disrupted during the attentional blink? An integrative review of event-related potential research._ Psychonomic Bulletin & Review, 2022;29:394–414. The review concludes that both disrupted attentional engagement and working-memory encoding contribute, while theoretical issues remain open. [Publisher page](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13423-021-01973-2).
3. Raymond JE, Shapiro KL, Arnell KM. _Temporary suppression of visual processing in an RSVP task: An attentional blink?_ Journal of Experimental Psychology: Human Perception and Performance, 1992;18:849–860. The seminal task used rapid serial visual presentation and supports the laboratory framing; cited and summarized in the reviews above.
